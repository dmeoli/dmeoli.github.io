Research

I came to optimization from symbolic AI, and more precisely from Knowledge Representation and Reasoning (KR&R), i.e., expert systems, logic programming, constraint satisfaction and SAT. In my view, the two fields share one premise: the difficulty of a problem depends (often to a large extent) on how it is written. Hence, the useful move is frequently to rewrite it, either as a set of smaller problems coordinated by a master or in a different formalism for which an efficient algorithm is available.

Symbolic AI. I started with expert systems and logic programming, for domains whose knowledge is codified and whose reasoning has to remain inspectable, and with ontologies as a representation of lexical knowledge. Of these, I have pursued further the translation between formalisms: a planning problem can be stated as a Constraint Satisfaction Problem and, from there, as a propositional formula, at which point the machinery of logical reasoning applies to it. I implemented that chain of translators in aima-python, together with the SAT solver at its end. The same question now comes back in the opposite direction: whether a satisfiability problem can be decomposed along its structure in the way one would decompose a mixed-integer program.

Numerical optimization. Decomposition performs the same operation on the structure of a model instead of on its formalism. This covers Lagrangian relaxation and Benders decomposition, the bundle methods that solve the nondifferentiable duals they give rise to, and the parallel solution of the subproblems, which are typically many and independent by construction. This is the premise of SMS++, where a model is declared as a tree of nested blocks; hence, the same instance can be solved monolithically, dualized along its linking constraints or split by scenario, and the choice among these is a matter of configuration that does not require the model to be rewritten. Reformulation comes back inside the subproblems, as the recognition of a structure for which a specialized (often combinatorial) algorithm exists. In fact, a decomposition method solves each subproblem many times, with data that change (usually rather little) from one call to the next. Much of my current work is therefore on re-optimization, i.e., on reusing what has been computed for one instance in the solution of the next one, and on enlarging the class of subproblems that dynamic programming can solve efficiently.

Machine learning, connecting the two. I see machine learning mainly as a way of learning heuristics, to be placed at the points where an exact algorithm makes a choice, and hence of making that algorithm faster. On the symbolic side this is the branching of a SAT solver (as in NeuroSAT); on the numerical side it is the branching of a branch-and-bound whose bounds come from a specialized relaxation, e.g., a Lagrangian one. In both cases the learned part makes the choice, while the guarantees still come from the exact method (at least as long as the learned choice is one that the method may take anyway); thus, a poor prediction may somewhat slow the method down without affecting the correctness of its result. Conversely, learning problems can be written as structured optimization models, to which the same decomposition and re-optimization machinery applies.

Publications

P1

Dynamic programming for the single-unit commitment problem with spinning reserves and reactive power

A. Frangioni, C. Gentile, D. Meoli

Submitted, 2026

Spinning reserves and reactive power are shown to be absorbable into the polynomial dynamic programming algorithms for the single-unit commitment (1UC) at essentially no algorithmic cost, by a per-period partial minimization that folds them into an effective convex piecewise-quadratic production cost. On the instances tested, the two resulting SMS++ solvers are about two orders of magnitude faster than a general-purpose MILP solver.
